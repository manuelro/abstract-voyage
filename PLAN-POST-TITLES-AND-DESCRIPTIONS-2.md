# Plan: Improve Post Titles and Descriptions for Information Scent

## Purpose

This document is a proposed editorial update plan for the titles and descriptions of the articles under `./posts/*.md`. It records the current copy, proposes revised copy where a revision improves information scent, and explains the reasoning behind each recommendation.

No post files were modified. No frontmatter, article body, or configuration was changed. This is a recommendation plan only.

The site renders two reader-facing fields from each post's frontmatter: `title` and `excerpt`. The analysis below treats `excerpt` as the article description. No article defines an explicit `id` or `slug` in frontmatter, so each article's ID is the Markdown filename without the `.md` extension, which is also the slug the site derives.

## Evaluation principles

Each recommendation was assessed against the following criteria:

- Information scent: does the title or description help a reader predict the article's relevance and content before opening it?
- Relevance judgment: can the reader quickly decide whether the article addresses their question, problem, or interest?
- Specificity: is abstract or generic wording replaced with a concrete problem, mechanism, framework, or payoff?
- Scannability: is the copy concise and information rich rather than padded or promotional?
- Differentiation: does the copy distinguish the article from neighboring articles in a small collection?
- Fidelity: is every claim grounded in the article itself, with no invented conclusions, frameworks, or audiences?
- Complementarity: does the description add information beyond the title rather than paraphrasing it?

Several titles and descriptions already satisfy these criteria. Where that is the case, the recommendation is to keep the existing copy, because the objective is higher information scent, not change for its own sake.

## Cross-article observations

1. The collection's titles are already strong overall. Most lead with a specific thesis, analogy, or named framework (for example the credit card analogy and the claim that the tech lead is a dependency to outgrow), which produces good information scent. The weaker points are descriptions, not titles.
2. Title and description overlap is the most common and most fixable issue. In the two leadership pieces and the welcome page, the description restates words already present in the title, which spends a limited proximal cue without improving the reader's ability to predict relevance.
3. Specificity is uneven between the engineering tutorials and the conceptual pieces. The engineering articles (SOLID, gzipping, DOM, feature toggles, dependency taxonomy) tend to name concrete mechanisms and payoffs, while some conceptual pieces describe the activity ("a conceptual exercise") rather than the reader's takeaway.
4. The two leadership articles risk competing for the same reader at a glance, since both foreground the tech lead role. Sharpening their descriptions (a personal retrospective of rewards and costs versus a structural argument for designing out the bottleneck) differentiates them without changing either title.
5. One description carries a grammatical error (the biomimetics excerpt), which undermines editorial credibility at the moment a reader is deciding whether to trust the article. Copy that functions as information scent deserves the same proofreading care as the article body.
